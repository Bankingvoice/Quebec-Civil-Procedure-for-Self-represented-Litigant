# Quebec Civil Procedure Guide for Self-Represented Litigants

Plain-language procedural companion based on Quebec's *Code of Civil Procedure* (CQLR, c. C-25.01). Translates legal rules into concrete steps for anyone representing themselves without prior legal training.

## When to Use

- The user wants to start a civil lawsuit, defend against one, or navigate a dispute in Quebec without a lawyer.
- The user asks which Quebec court handles their dispute (Small Claims, Court of Québec, Superior Court, or specialized tribunals like the TAL).
- The user needs help understanding Quebec procedural steps: formal notices (*mise en demeure*), pleadings, notification/service, case protocols, discovery, trial preparation, appeals, or collecting a judgment.
- The user asks about procedural deadlines, page limits, or self-representation rules under the C.C.P.

## Core Principles for Assisting Self-Represented Litigants

1. **Conclusion first, then steps and citations:** Give the direct procedural answer upfront, followed by actionable steps, exact article numbers (e.g., art. 23 C.C.P.), and verifiable public resources (LégisQuébec, Ministère de la Justice du Québec, Éducaloi).
2. **Procedural information, not legal advice:** Explain *how* the rules work and what options exist; remind the user that court clerks can explain forms and filing rules but cannot give legal advice or calculate prescription deadlines (art. 23 C.C.P.).
3. **Proportionality and good faith:** Emphasize that Quebec procedure requires parties to keep steps proportional to the amount and complexity at stake (art. 18 C.C.P.), cooperate and share relevant facts/evidence (art. 20 C.C.P.), and consider private dispute resolution (negotiation, mediation, arbitration) before going to court (art. 1 C.C.P.).
4. **Equal application of rules:** Self-represented individuals have the right to represent themselves (art. 23 C.C.P.), but judges hold them to the same procedural rules and deadlines as lawyers. Corporations generally must be represented by a lawyer outside Small Claims (art. 87 C.C.P.).

## Step-by-Step Workflow

### Step 1: Triage Forum and Monetary Jurisdiction

Determine where the case belongs before drafting anything. Refer to `references/ccp-structure-and-forums.md` for full details.

- **Specialized Tribunals (Check First):** Residential leases go to the *Tribunal administratif du logement* (TAL); workplace injuries/labour go to CNESST / TAT; human rights or administrative appeals go to their respective tribunals, not ordinary civil courts.
- **Small Claims Division (Court of Québec) - $15,000 or less (arts. 536–570 C.C.P.):**
  - Principal claimed cannot exceed $15,000 (excluding interest). You may permanently waive the excess to stay in Small Claims, but you cannot split one debt into multiple claims (art. 536–537 C.C.P.).
  - **No lawyers allowed** at the hearing for either side (with narrow exceptions, art. 542 C.C.P.).
  - Businesses/partnerships can only file as plaintiffs if they had **10 or fewer employees** over the past 12 months (art. 536 C.C.P.).
- **Court of Québec (Civil Division) - Under $75,000 exclusive; $75,000 to under $100,000 concurrent (art. 35 C.C.P.):**
  - Handles civil claims below $75,000 exclusively, and shares jurisdiction with Superior Court from $75,000 up to $100,000 (amounts subject to statutory indexing; verify current indexed thresholds on LégisQuébec).
  - Claims under $100,000 in Court of Québec follow **Special Simplified Rules** (Book VI, Title I.1, arts. 535.1–535.15 C.C.P.): no case protocol, strict 5-page limit on originating applications, early exhibit disclosure, and tighter discovery limits.
- **Superior Court of Québec - $100,000+ or reserved matters (art. 33 C.C.P.):**
  - Default court for claims of $100,000 or more, injunctions, class actions, judicial review, and family law matters (divorce, custody, support).

### Step 2: Check Prescription (Limitation Period) and Pre-Filing Steps

- **Prescription check:** Confirm the legal deadline to sue has not expired under the *Civil Code of Québec* (generally **3 years** for most contractual and civil liability claims, art. 2925 C.C.Q., though shorter deadlines apply to municipalities, defamation, or bodily injury).
- **Formal Notice (*Mise en demeure*):** Strongly recommended before suing (and legally required in certain cases to put the other party in default and start interest running, art. 1594 C.C.Q.). State what is demanded, a reasonable deadline (typically 10–15 days), and send it with proof of receipt (registered mail, bailiff, or email with confirmation).
- **Private Dispute Resolution (arts. 1–7 C.C.P.):** Document that you considered negotiation or mediation before filing.

### Step 3: Draft and File the Originating Application

- **Small Claims:** Complete official Form **SJ-870E** (online via Ministère de la Justice or at the courthouse registry), attach supporting exhibits, and pay the tariff fee. The court clerk serves the defendant by registered mail (art. 547 C.C.P.).
- **Court of Québec / Superior Court:** Draft an **Originating Application** (*Demande introductive d'instance*, arts. 141–145 C.C.P.) with a **Summons** (*Avis d'assignation*, art. 145 C.C.P.).
  - Number every paragraph; state one fact per paragraph; state exact conclusions (what you want the judge to order).
  - Respect page limits (e.g., 5 pages max under simplified Court of Québec rules, art. 535.2 C.C.P.).
  - Pay court filing fees and obtain a court record number from the clerk (*greffe*).

### Step 4: Notify (Serve) the Other Party Properly

- **Service vs. Notification (arts. 109–140 C.C.P.):** Originating applications in Court of Québec and Superior Court generally require formal **service by a bailiff** (*signification par huissier*, art. 139 C.C.P.). Subsequent documents can usually be sent by **notification** (registered mail, courier, or technological means/email where permitted, arts. 110, 133 C.C.P.).
- **Proof of notification:** Keep the bailiff's return of service or delivery receipt; the court cannot proceed without proof the other side received notice.
- **Deadlines after service:** Once served, the defendant generally has **15 days** to file an **Answer** (*Réponse*, art. 147 C.C.P.) indicating whether they will contest or settle.

### Step 5: Manage Pre-Trial Steps, Evidence, and Deadlines

Refer to `references/deadlines-and-checklists.md` for time-counting rules and document prep.

- **Case Protocol vs. Simplified Track:**
  - In Superior Court (and Court of Québec claims of $100,000+), parties must file a joint **Case Protocol** (*Protocole de l'instance*, art. 148 C.C.P.) within **45 days** of service, setting dates for defence, examinations, and trial readiness.
  - In Court of Québec simplified proceedings (under $100,000, arts. 535.1–535.15 C.C.P.), no case protocol is required; strict statutory timelines govern disclosure, the 2-page summary defence, and settlement conferences.
- **Exhibits (*Pièces*):** Label every piece of evidence sequentially (`P-1`, `P-2` for plaintiff; `D-1`, `D-2` for defendant), list them in an **Exhibit List** (*Inventaire des pièces*), and disclose copies to the other party before filing/trial.
- **Discovery (Examinations):** Pre-trial oral or written examinations are prohibited in Small Claims and in Court of Québec cases under $50,000 (art. 229 C.C.P.). Joint expert reports are mandatory for disputes under $50,000 unless the court authorizes otherwise (art. 535.10 C.C.P.).

### Step 6: Prepare for the Hearing, Judgment, and Enforcement

- **Hearing preparation:** Organize a chronological fact sheet, bring 3 copies of all exhibits (one for the judge, one for the other party, one for yourself), and ensure witnesses are notified or subpoenaed (*citation à comparaître*, art. 269 C.C.P.) at least 10 days before the hearing.
- **Courtroom rules (arts. 14–15 C.C.P.):** Address the judge as "Justice" or "Your Honour" (*Monsieur le Juge / Madame la Juge*), speak calmly, stick to relevant facts, and never record audio/video without authorization.
- **After Judgment (Book IV & Book VIII C.C.P.):**
  - Small Claims judgments are **final and cannot be appealed** (art. 564 C.C.P.).
  - Other civil judgments generally have a **30-day appeal window** from the date of the notice of judgment (art. 360 C.C.P.; leave to appeal may be required).
  - Winning a judgment does not automatically put money in your hand. If the debtor does not pay voluntarily (usually within 30 days, or 10 days in Small Claims), use **Execution of Judgments** (Book VIII, arts. 656–777 C.C.P.) via a bailiff to seize wages, bank accounts, or property.

## Common Pitfalls and Gotchas

- **Suing the wrong legal name:** Always verify a business's exact legal name on the *Registraire des entreprises du Québec* (REQ), not just its storefront brand name.
- **Missing the 15-day Answer or 45-day Protocol:** Failing to file an Answer within 15 days allows the plaintiff to seek a **default judgment** (art. 180 C.C.P.).
- **Counting days incorrectly (art. 83 C.C.P.):** Exclude the first day (day of service/event) and include the last day. If the last day falls on a weekend or legal holiday, it extends to the next working day. Saturdays count when calculating deadlines, except when the deadline ends on a Saturday.
- **Confusing substantive law with procedure:** The *Code of Civil Procedure* tells you *how* to bring a claim; the *Civil Code of Québec* (C.C.Q.) establishes *whether* you have a legal right (contract, fault, damages).
